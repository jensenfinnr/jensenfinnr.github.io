1954 Chevrolet Corvette

A summer on a first-generation Corvette, working directly with a builder who has done this many times before.

The job

Leri Automotive took on a full restomod of a 1954 Chevrolet Corvette. I came in as the design and fabrication intern and worked next to builder John Leri through the build.

A car this old doesn't arrive with drawings you can use, and after seventy years no two panels sit exactly where the factory left them. So every part started the same way: measure the car itself, then turn those dimensions into a fabrication plan.

What I made

  • Electronics access panel. The piece I own on this car. It covers the electronics in the rear quarter, and a channel recessed into its perimeter carries a 5 mm LED strip — so the panel doesn't get lit, the panel is the light. It had to seal to the body, hide the hardware, still open for service, and hold a consistent gap for the strip all the way around.
  • Brackets and mounts. Custom sheet-metal brackets to support assembly and tie components into the car.
  • SolidWorks models. The panel and the other custom parts were modeled and dimensioned in CAD before anything got cut.
  • Second set of hands. A lot of a build is holding, aligning, and checking while someone else fits or welds. I was on the car for that too, and it's where most of the learning happened.
  • The film. I shot and edited the video documenting the finished build.

What it taught me

The distance between a model and a part is measurement. A model that quietly assumes symmetry will hand you a panel that doesn't fit a hand-built body, and you find out at the worst possible moment. Everything I made got better once I stopped trusting the drawing and started trusting the tape.

The LED channel made that concrete. A 5 mm strip in a recess only looks right if the gap is even the whole way around — a millimetre of drift over a long run reads as a mistake from across the room, even to someone who couldn't tell you why.
