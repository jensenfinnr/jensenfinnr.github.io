BMW F10 · Electronics retrofit

F10 Interior
Lighting

Platform: BMW F10

New lighting in an older interior, controlled from a switch that looks like it came with the car.

The goal

Bolt-on interior lighting kits are easy to spot: strips stuck to trim, a wire disappearing under a mat, a remote in the glovebox. I wanted the opposite — light with no visible source, and one control on the dash that a passenger would assume was factory.

Mechanical

The hard part isn't the LEDs, it's holding them in the right place. I modeled and printed a clip that clamps around the light tube on one side and snaps into existing features in the trim on the other, so every run sits at a fixed angle and throws light onto a surface instead of into your eyes.

Printing the clips meant I could revise one, print it that night, and try again — and it kept the whole install reversible. Nothing is drilled, nothing is glued, and the car can go back to stock.

Electrical and control

  • Bench test first. Every run got powered up and checked outside the car before a single panel came off. Finding a dead segment on the floor costs minutes; finding it behind a door card costs an evening.
  • Harness. Wiring routed behind the trim and tied into the car's 12V system at the fuse panel, with connectors at each run so a panel can still come off without cutting.
  • Switch. A control integrated into the dash rather than an app or a fob. Reach for it without looking.
  • Programming. I wrote the control logic so the same switch sets both brightness and color, which is the interesting constraint: one physical input, two dimensions of output, and it has to be obvious to use while driving.

What it taught me

Retrofit work is mostly a packaging problem. The electronics were straightforward; making them disappear into a car that was designed without them is where the engineering was.
